How to save a web page with SingleFile and never lose it.

Last update: 25/02/2026
Author Isaac

Mundobytes » Software » How to save a web page with SingleFile and never lose it

  • SingleFile allows you to save entire web pages in a single standard HTML file, portable and compatible with almost all browsers.
  • The extension offers advanced features such as saving multiple tabs, automatic mode, annotations, and direct upload to Google Drive.
  • Additional plugins and services (Scrapbook, public web archives, search engine caches) help organize and retrieve content even when the original site disappears.

If you often save interesting links in bookmarks, reading lists, or apps like Pocket, you've probably experienced the disappointment of reopening an old link only to find an error message saying the page no longer exists . That feeling of "I had it here and it's gone" is more common than you might think.

The internet changes at breakneck speed; websites are updated daily, and content that seems essential today can disappear without warning . Servers crash, websites are abandoned, articles are deleted by mistake or by the author—the reasons are endless. Therefore, if you want to ensure you don't lose anything important, the best strategy is to create your own offline copies of the pages you're interested in.

Why it's worth saving web pages with SingleFile

The idea of ​​saving content for offline viewing isn't new: we've been talking about saving pages as PDFs , taking screenshots, or using reading services for years . What's different with SingleFile is the convenience and the quality of the result. This extension lets you save an entire web page in a single HTML file , including text, styles, images, fonts, and even some embedded elements, without needing any auxiliary folders.

When you use your browser's classic "Save Page As..." option, clicking "Save Page As..." generates an HTML file along with a folder containing images, stylesheets, and other resources. This system works, but it's fragile: if you move the file without the associated folder, rename something, or copy only the HTML, the file paths break, and the page no longer looks the same.

SingleFile tackles this problem by packaging everything into a single document. It uses, among other technical tricks, the data:URI scheme to embed images , CSS, and other resources directly into the HTML. The result is a compact file that's easy to move, copy, email, or save to the cloud, and it maintains a near-identical clone of the original page.

Furthermore, we live in a time when saving content offline is making sense again. Although we're almost always connected these days, when we travel, work in areas with poor coverage, or experience network outages , having your critical pages downloaded can be a lifesaver. Just like you do with videos on YouTube Go or downloadable content on streaming platforms, you can do the same with your favorite websites.

How SingleFile works and which browsers it's available in

SingleFile is an extension developed by Gildas Lormeau and is available for most modern browsers: Firefox, Chrome, Edge, and Opera , as well as other Chromium-based browsers. It installs like any other extension from the relevant store and adds a button shaped like a sheet of paper with a down arrow to the toolbar.

Basic operation is straightforward: wait for the page to load completely and click the SingleFile button . The extension processes the content, integrates all the necessary resources, and in a few seconds presents you with the download of a single HTML file. If you change your mind while it's processing, you can click the same button again to cancel.

Once saved, you can open that file in virtually any modern browser: Chrome, Firefox, Edge, Opera, Safari, Konqueror … even some older browsers will display at least the main content. You don't need to reinstall SingleFile to read the files: they are self-contained HTML pages , not a proprietary format or a closed database.

It's important to understand that SingleFile isn't a massive website crawler . It's not designed to download an entire domain at once, but rather to carefully save the page you have open . To clone entire websites, other solutions are still used, such as WebCopier or other site downloaders, which crawl links and download multiple pages.

In return, SingleFile focuses on what matters: faithfully capturing the appearance and content necessary for the page to function and not display visible errors . To prevent the file size from skyrocketing, it won't always save absolutely all secondary resources, but only what's needed to preserve the overall structure.

Advanced options: context menu, multiple tabs, and automatic mode

Beyond the quick icon click, SingleFile offers a powerful context menu . Right-clicking the extension button or a page reveals specific options for saving content in various ways . This makes it easy to integrate into your daily workflow without any hassle.

From that menu, you can choose to save the current tab, only a selected portion of the page, a specific frame, or one or more selected links . For example, if you have a link to documents or articles, you can select them and ask SingleFile to process them in batches, instead of one by one.

Another key feature is the ability to work with multiple tabs simultaneously . The extension allows you to process and save all tabs, only unpinned tabs, or only selected tabs with a single click . This is incredibly useful if, for example, you're researching a topic with ten pages open and want to keep them as is to review them offline later.

In addition, SingleFile includes an automatic save mode . Once activated, it automatically saves pages as they finish loading , so you don't have to remember to click the button each time. In Chrome, this mode is activated directly from the extension's context menu; in Firefox, you need to install an additional add-on called SingleFile Auto-Save , which adds a separate icon to enable or disable this continuous saving.

As if that weren't enough, the developer has added a customizable keyboard shortcut (default Ctrl+Shift+Y) that allows you to save the current tab or a group of selected tabs on the fly, further speeding up the process once you've become accustomed to using the extension.

SingleFile customization and useful settings

SingleFile's default settings are usually more than enough for most users , but if you want to fine-tune things, the extension offers a very comprehensive options panel. You can open it by right-clicking on the icon and choosing "Manage extension" or "Options ," depending on your browser.

Within that panel, you can modify, for example, the length and format of the generated file names . If you save many pages, you might prefer shorter names, or to always include the date and time in a specific format to better organize your offline library.

It also lets you choose how to handle duplicate resources like images and fonts . If you enable duplicate removal, the resulting file can be somewhat smaller, as SingleFile will avoid embedding the same resource twice. Similarly, you can decide whether to remove audio and video sources that aren't essential for reading, which is useful if you prioritize file size over absolute fidelity.

Another interesting setting is the option to save the page "raw ," meaning without the browser executing JavaScript before capturing it. This mode can be useful for very dynamic, complex pages, or pages with problematic scripts, since you save the HTML exactly as it arrives from the server , without prior transformation.

Among the most striking advanced features is the ability to automatically upload saved pages to Google Drive . If you activate the "Misc. > save to Google Drive" option, each screenshot can be copied directly to your cloud storage, ideal if you want to sync your file offline across computers or always have an extra backup copy.

There's even a feature to add "proof of existence" for the saved page to the blockchain . By enabling "Misc. > add proof of existence," the extension calculates the file's SHA256 hash and links it to the blockchain, which can be useful for proving that certain content existed on a specific date , for example, in legal or investigative contexts.

Annotate, clean, and organize saved pages

SingleFile doesn't just save what you see on screen; it also offers a quick editing option before saving. Using the context menu entry "Annotate and save the page…" , you can highlight text, add notes, and remove unnecessary elements (banners, menus, comment blocks, etc.) before generating the final HTML file.

This makes SingleFile more than just a simple copying system: it transforms into a collection and study tool . You can keep only the relevant part of the page, highlight important ideas, and leave comments directly embedded in the document, which you can then open offline.

If you want to take your organization to the next level, there's an add-on called Scrapbook for SingleFile . Instead of asking you for a destination folder each time, this add-on saves processed pages to an internal database accessible directly from your browser . What you get is a kind of "archive" or library of SingleFile pages, organized in a list.

From that list, you can open any saved page, manage them, delete them, or, if you prefer, export an individual file . To do this, simply open it in your browser and use the normal save function (for example, with the classic Ctrl+S shortcut ) to the folder of your choice.

It's important to remember that Scrapbook requires SingleFile to be installed , as it relies on it to generate HTML copies. SingleFile, on the other hand, works perfectly well on its own: you can use it without Scrapbook, save your files wherever you want, and open them later in other browsers without needing any extra extensions.

Save all tabs in a single HTML file

In addition to saving each page in its own file, there are situations where it can be very useful to group several tabs into a single HTML file . Think, for example, when you're planning a trip, studying a complex topic, or comparing products, and you have a lot of tabs open related to that subject.

SingleFile, especially in its version for Chrome and compatible browsers, includes specific options for managing all the tabs in the window . Through the context menu when you right-click on the icon, you can choose between saving the active tab, a set of selected tabs, or all open tabs . This way, you generate a single HTML file that groups the entire browsing session.

Imagine you're planning a trip and you have tabs open with tourist information, maps, reservations, and reviews of hotels and restaurants . You can select only the tabs related to the trip and save them in a single HTML file, so that days later, even offline, you can reopen that file and have your entire trip on a single page.

This approach is also useful for users who work on projects . Each project can correspond to a single file that groups all the references consulted. This way, you don't depend on individual bookmarks or rely on websites still being online in a few months.

If you ever get tired of the extension or no longer need it, uninstalling it is as easy as right-clicking the icon and choosing Remove/Extensions > Uninstall . HTML files you've already saved will continue to work, as they don't require SingleFile to be installed to open.

SingleFile versus other methods for saving pages

The SingleFile extension isn't the only way to save web content for offline reading , but it is one of the most versatile when you want to preserve the overall appearance of the page. Even so, it's worth comparing it with other alternatives to understand when to use each option.

The browser itself offers several native options: on the one hand, saving only the HTML , which essentially stores the page's text without graphics or sophisticated styles; on the other, the "full web page" option , which creates the familiar file + folder combo with all the included resources. Some browsers also support the MHTML (.mhtml) format , which packages everything into a single file, but its compatibility is not universal.

Compared to these alternatives, SingleFile offers a standard HTML file, compatible with almost all modern browsers , and avoids the proliferation of auxiliary folders. Furthermore, it adds advantages such as annotation, multiple tab saving, and integration with Google Drive or blockchain proofs of existence—features that native options lack.

Another very common method is to print the page to PDF . This technique is great when you're only interested in the main content and want to remove menus, sidebars, and banners . PDFs are easy to share and look the same on virtually any device. However, in return, you lose interactivity, active links, and some of the original page structure.

If you're simply looking for a readable copy without being distracted by unnecessary elements, services like Pocket, Wallabag, or Raindrop.io are very convenient. You send them the link, they download the page, clean it up, and save it to your personal cloud storage. Then you can organize the pages by tags, search within the text, and access them from any device. However, in many cases, unlimited archiving features are paid , and you also depend on the service remaining operational.

What happens when a page disappears: public archives and caches

No matter how organized you are, there will always be times when you didn't manage to save something with SingleFile in time. When a website goes down or an article disappears, you still have some options to recover previous versions of the pages.

The best-known resource is the Internet Archive and its Wayback Machine (archive.org). This project has been archiving millions of websites for years. To find out if the page you're interested in is saved, simply go to web.archive.org and enter the URL in the "Save page now" box or in the general search.

If the page has already been archived, you'll see a calendar with the dates and years when screenshots were taken . Select a date, wait for it to load, and you'll access a historical version of that website. The system also displays a graph that allows you to easily jump to older or newer copies. Each screenshot has its own permanent link, which includes the exact date and time it was archived.

You can also ask the Wayback Machine to archive the current page right then and there using the "Save page now" form. The tool crawls the web, downloads its main components, and stores a public copy that anyone can access in the future, even if the original website disappears.

Other similar projects include archive.today (archive.is), perma.cc, and services like megalodon.jp . Archive.today, for example, doesn't automatically crawl the web; it only saves pages at the request of users . This allows it to index content that other crawlers don't access, such as certain pages excluded by robots.txt.

Beyond specialized archives, there has always been the option of using cached pages from search engines . When indexing, Google, Bing, and other search engines save a text version (sometimes with formatting) of the page. For years, Google provided a direct link to this cache in search results; today it has largely hidden it and it is much less accessible, but shortcuts and extensions still exist that help access it.

Extensions and services for working with archived content

Manually searching for old copies can be tedious, so extensions designed to simplify access to archived websites have emerged . One of the most practical options is the "Web Archives" extension, which detects when a link leads to a broken page and automatically redirects you to its copy on the Wayback Machine, archive.today, perma.cc , or even the cached versions on Google, Bing, or Yandex.

With these extensions installed, browsing becomes smoother when working with old links, bibliographies, academic articles, or news items that tend to disappear or move to different sites. If the server becomes unresponsive, the tool automatically checks for available files and offers an alternative without you having to manually try each service.

If you're into serious documentation, perma.cc might interest you: it's powered by a consortium of major libraries and is designed to generate stable links that can be cited in articles, theses, or legal rulings. The catch is that it's not free for everyone : member organizations have broader access, while individual users have to pay for plans with limits on the number of archived links.

Alongside these solutions, it's worth noting that many note-taking apps also integrate web clippers . Evernote Web Clipper, for example, captures the page, cleans it up, and saves it directly to your notebook. While not as faithful to the original layout as SingleFile, it's very convenient when you want to combine web content with your own notes and organization.

Whichever method you use (SingleFile, archiving services, search engine caches, or note clippers), the key is to understand one thing clearly: what's online today may not still be online tomorrow . The sooner you integrate some form of archiving into your routine, the less you'll depend on the goodwill of others to preserve what matters to you.

With all of the above, it's clear that SingleFile has earned its place as one of the most practical ways to save entire web pages in a single, reliable, portable, and compatible HTML file . Whether for offline reading, documenting changes, preserving resources that have helped you for years, or protecting yourself against the "link decay" that gradually empties the web, combining SingleFile with public archiving services, caching tools, and organizational systems allows you to create your own "personal internet" that's resilient to power outages, unexpected shutdowns, and others' forgetfulness.
